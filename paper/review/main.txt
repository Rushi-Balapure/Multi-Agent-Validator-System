                  Multi-Agent Validator System
        Independent Evidence for Scientific Claim Verification



                                                    Abstract
          False endorsement is a costly error in scientific claim checking. We test whether searching beyond
      the supplied citations reduces it. The validator stages four isolated roles over two evidence scopes:
      a sealed reader that answers a neutral question from retrieved passages before seeing the claim,
      a claim-visible judge over four labels (supported, contradicted, unaddressed, underdetermined), a
      citation auditor over the original references, and a reconciler that reports the two scopes separately
      instead of collapsing them into one score. Every stage fails closed: a transport or parse failure is an
      execution status, never a scientific verdict.
          On SciFact, we compare the system with a baseline that already reads the original citations.
      Across 300 development claims, the baseline incorrectly endorses about 1 in 100 claims whose
      gold label is not support; the staged validator makes no such endorsement. Yet it finds only 43%
      of supported claims, compared with 57% for the baseline. A separate 300-claim held-out sample
      shows the same trade-off, with 38 validator requests failing closed. The reduction is not statistically
      significant. We replicate the comparison with a MiniMax family and on a frozen thirty-claim subset
      with four models, all closed-weight. The direction is stable: false endorsement never rises, and
      the cost appears as withheld contradictions. Disagreement between evidence scopes drives many
      abstentions. In this benchmark, every annotated gold document is already among the cited documents,
      limiting what independent retrieval can add. We report the negative result with a mechanism analysis
      and cost accounting. The system offers more conservative verdicts, but misses more supported claims.

Artifact. All tables and figures in this paper are generated from saved prediction files by the scripts in
scripts/paper/. The corpus, splits, prompts, and frozen configurations are in the accompanying
repository (Appendix F lists the commands), and the held-out split is protected by a hash freeze that
refuses a run whose model, prompt, configuration, or corpus differs from the locked record.


1    Introduction
Large language models are now asked to check claims, not only to make them. In the usual pipeline
a checker sees a claim and the citations offered with it, then returns a verdict. It therefore inherits the
generator’s evidence: if the generator cited the wrong paper, or cited a paper that supports a slightly
different claim, the checker reads the same biased evidence set and cannot notice. The failure is a property
of the information it is given, not a bug in the checker.
     One remedy is to have the checker search for its own evidence. Recent work in retrieval-augmented
factuality evaluation [Wei et al., 2024], in self-check agents [Li et al., 2026, Hirsch et al., 2026], and in
retrieval-augmented verifiers [Yue et al., 2024, Zheng et al., 2024] points in that direction. Independent
retrieval plus careful staging is widely assumed to help. It has rarely been tested against a strong
same-evidence baseline on scientific claims, where the original citations are often correct and where an
abstention can be the right answer.
     This paper tests the assumption directly. We build a staged validator (V) that separates four roles
(a sealed evidence reader, a claim-visible judge, a citation auditor, and a reconciler) over two evidence
scopes: the original citations (D0 ) and independently retrieved abstracts (D1 ). We compare it against a
same-evidence baseline (B2) that reads a neutral question over the original citations in an isolated reader
and then compares, which is the strongest same-evidence configuration we could build without training.

We also build the full factorial around the two interventions we care about: evidence access (D0 versus
D1 ) and reading policy (claim-visible versus neutral-question evidence-first).
    Our preregistered primary outcome is false endorsement: the rate at which a method predicts
“supported” for a claim that the gold labels do not support. The observed answer is inconclusive. On
the frozen development sample the paired difference between V and B2 is -0.011 with a 95% interval
of [-0.029, 0.000], which includes zero. The held-out sample repeats the pattern. The staged validator
does reach zero false endorsements, but it buys that by withholding support: SUPPORT recall drops
from 0.573 to 0.427 on the development sample, and on the held-out sample 38 of 300 claims fail closed
entirely. Both false endorsement and support recall are needed to judge the trade-off.
    Three findings frame the result.
 • Independent retrieval is not the bottleneck; scope reconciliation is. The failure analysis shows that
   the dominant mode of V is not a retrieval miss but a disagreement between the D0 and D1 judgments,
   which the reconciler resolves conservatively into “underdetermined”. When the two scopes agree, V
   inherits the baseline’s accuracy; when they disagree, V abstains. In practice the system behaves as a
   disagreement detector.
 • Staged judgment buys precision, not overall accuracy. Micro-F1 falls because recall falls faster
   than precision rises. Reported as F1 alone, this looks like a regression (0.747 against 0.687); reported
   as a precision–coverage trade-off it is a usable operating point for a conservative reader-facing tool.
 • The direction repeats across models. We repeat the pairing on a fixed thirty-claim subset with four
   instruction-tuned models, all closed-weight. False endorsement never rises under staging, and the cost
   lands on contradiction rather than on support: REFUTE recall falls in all four models while SUPPORT
   recall barely moves. The subset is small, so this is a statement about direction, not significance.
    No human study was run: no participant was recruited, no ethics review was obtained, and we make
no claim about human trust. The four-way labels are described but not scored, because SciFact’s label
space is three-way and has no annotation for the distinction. The baselines are prompt-level adaptations
of the named mechanisms, not reproductions of trained verifiers. Finally, our corpus is a fixed set of
biomedical abstracts, so every claim of support is relative to that corpus and retrieval budget.
    The contribution is a reproducible negative result with a mechanism rather than a new score: a
measurement of what independent retrieval and evidence-first staging do, and do not do, to a scientific
claim checker, together with the artifact that makes it checkable.


2     Related Work
2.1   Verification datasets and scientific claim checking
FEVER [Thorne et al., 2018] established the claim–evidence verification format that this paper follows,
and FEVEROUS [Aly et al., 2021] extended it to unstructured and structured evidence. SciFact [Wadden
et al., 2020] is the benchmark used here: it pairs scientific claims with abstracts and rationale sentences,
and supplies support, refute, and insufficient-information labels. SciFact-Open [Wadden et al., 2022]
extends the task to open-domain retrieval, which motivates our separation of a frozen corpus from live
search; we use a frozen corpus only, and we do not claim open-domain performance. Missci [Glockner
et al., 2024] targets misrepresented science and fallacy reconstruction rather than four-way claim labeling,
and RAGTruth [Niu et al., 2024] targets context faithfulness of retrieval-augmented generation rather than
evidence sufficiency of a checker; we cite both as boundaries of our setting rather than as benchmarks
we score. For biomedical claims specifically, SciClaims [Ortega and Gomez-Perez, 2025] provides an
end-to-end generative pipeline; we treat it as prior art for scientific verification with explanations and
do not reproduce it. Vladika and Matthes [Vladika and Matthes, 2024] compare knowledge sources for
open-domain scientific claim verification and motivate our interest in whether a different evidence source
changes verdicts.


                                                     2
2.2   LLM verifiers, self-checking agents, and retrieval
A long line of work asks an LLM to judge claims. Chain-of-Verification [Dhuliawala et al., 2024]
isolates the verification questions from the draft answer, which is the closest published analogue to our
information separation and which our B2 baseline deliberately imitates. Safe [Wei et al., 2024] verifies
atomic facts with search and is the reference point for our fresh retrieval arm. Self-checking agents such
as MARCH [Li et al., 2026, Qwen-Applications, 2026] add information separation between proposer and
checker, and blame-localization work [Hirsch et al., 2026] shows that trace-level attribution of citation
errors is a developer-facing diagnostic distinct from evaluating a final conclusion for an end user; we
evaluate final conclusions, and cite the trace work as the reason not to claim it. A systematic auditor
for retrieval-augmented generation [Yu et al., 2026] and retrieval-augmented verifiers such as RAFTS
[Yue et al., 2024] and RAV [Zheng et al., 2024] further motivate separating evidence discovery from
judgment. Explainable fact checking through question answering [Yang et al., 2022] and hallucination
detection methods such as SelfCheckGPT [Manakul et al., 2023] and RARR [Gao et al., 2023] address
the neighbouring problem of detecting unsupported content in model output.
    Little in the design is new in isolation. Atomic decomposition, blinded checking, search-based
verification, and readable explanations each have substantial prior art. Our contribution is a controlled
measurement of their combination against a strong same-evidence control, and a negative result about
that combination.

2.3   Abstention, calibration, and selective use
A checker that cannot abstain will answer when it should not. Work on abstention [Feng et al., 2024]
and on calibrated selective fact checking [Yang, 2026] formalises the trade-off we measure. Calibration
theory [Guo et al., 2017] distinguishes a model’s verbalised confidence from a probability fitted on
held-out data; we fit a single temperature on a calibration split and report raw and calibrated numbers
separately. Because a scientific “underdetermined” verdict and a system-level “defer to a reviewer” action
are different things, we keep them in separate fields. On the human side, confidence and explanation are
known to affect both accuracy and trust calibration in AI-assisted decisions [Zhang et al., 2020], so we do
not claim that human trust calibration is unstudied; we did not run the study and we report the protocol
without results.


3     Problem Formulation
3.1   Inputs and evidence scopes
Let c be an atomic scientific claim taken from a frozen report. A claim carries an exact source span,
a normalised statement, and a neutral question: a single interrogative formulation of the claim that
preserves population, comparator, units, and causal scope but does not contain the asserted answer. Two
evidence scopes are available:
• D0 — the documents the original report cited. These are the generator’s evidence. A judgment over
  D0 answers: is the original citation set faithful to the claim?
• D1 — documents retrieved independently from a frozen corpus using the neutral question, with the
  claim’s answer, the report, and D0 hidden from the retrieval and reading stages. A judgment over D1
  answers: what does independent retrieval establish?
    A verdict is always relative to a stated scope, corpus, and search budget. It is not an unrestricted
declaration of scientific truth. The reconciled judgment over D0 ∪ D1 answers: what can be said once
both scopes are considered?




                                                    3
3.2   Four labels
Internally the system uses four labels, defined relative to a specified evidence collection:

Supported
  Relevant evidence establishes the exact scoped assertion and no unresolved material counterevidence
  remains in the reviewed pool.

Contradicted
  Relevant evidence establishes an incompatible assertion; apparent conflicts have been resolved by
  scope or relevance.

Unaddressed
  No reviewed relevant passage answers the claim under the stated corpus and retrieval budget.

Underdetermined
  Relevant evidence exists, but disagreement, imprecision, incomplete measurement, or an unsupported
  inference prevents resolution.

    The decision order is: check execution status and claim validity; then assess relevance; if no passage
addresses the claim, use unaddressed; if relevant evidence is materially conflicting or inconclusive,
use underdetermined; otherwise apply support or contradiction, but only at the preserved scope. A
non-significant result is not automatically proof of no effect, and a null-result claim requires its own
evidence assessment.
    SciFact’s native label space is three-way, so the scored mapping is supported → SUPPORT, contra-
dicted → REFUTE, and unaddressed or underdetermined → NEI. The four-way distinction is therefore
reported but not scored against gold. We say this once here and repeat it wherever a number could be
misread.

3.3   Research questions
• RQ1 (primary, preregistered). At a matched inference budget, does independent retrieval plus staged
  evidence-first judgment reduce false endorsement relative to same-evidence checking?
• RQ2 (descriptive). Can the system distinguish missing evidence from unresolved evidence, and how
  often does it defer?
• RQ3 (deferred). Do evidence-linked explanations improve non-experts’ judgments, including when
  the validator is wrong? No participant was recruited; Appendix D gives the protocol and no results.
• RQ4 (secondary). How do accuracy, coverage, latency, and cost change across generator/checker
  families and across the two interventions?

4.1   Roles and information boundaries
Table 1 gives the procedure for one claim and the evidence scope each step may read.

Proposer.
  Maps a report to atomic claims, neutral questions, asserted answers, and exact source spans. Quantities,
  confidence language, population, comparator, and causal scope are preserved; a conjunction is split
  while the relation between its parts is retained.

Independent gatherer.
  Given the neutral question, a scope, and the corpus, retrieves fresh evidence. The asserted answer, the
  report, the original citations, and the generator’s identity are hidden. Three query forms are issued
  per claim (open inquiry; scope and measurement; limitations or null results); the top candidates are
  deduplicated and reranked, and up to eight passages are kept.

Evidence reader.
  Answers the neutral question from the shuffled retrieved passages and cites the passage identifiers it
  used. Its record is sealed before the claim is revealed.

    The blinding is partial: a neutral question still reveals the topic and may leak assumptions. The
isolation guarantee is structural, not semantic. The reader’s payload contains only the neutral question
and passage text, and an assertion on the payload shape plus a test suite enforce that.

4.2   Execution and stopping
The report is generated once and frozen. Validation does not rewrite it. A claim budget of eight claims
per report, two retrieval rounds, three query forms per round, and eight passages in the judge context are
development defaults that were frozen before the held-out run. A second retrieval round is permitted only
for unresolved questions, scope mismatch, or contradictory evidence; a run stops on exhausted budget, no
new eligible documents, or a complete bounded evidence record. Every intermediate artifact is persisted,
and the runner resumes at the last completed claim without mixing configurations.
    The system fails closed by default. When the model returns malformed JSON, cites a passage it was
not given, or the endpoint fails, the system records an execution status of failed or timeout and a
placeholder label that is a schema token, never model text. These rows are excluded from scored metrics
and reported separately. They are never silently remapped into a scientific verdict.

4.3   Reconciliation
Agreement between scopes copies the shared label. A disagreement, or a failed D0 audit, resolves to
underdetermined and hence to NEI on the scored label space. We do not repair citation faithfulness with

                                                        6
outside evidence: a claim can be unsupported by its own citation and supported elsewhere, and the two
facts are reported separately.


5     Implementation
5.1   Records and reproducibility contract
The implementation emits a machine-readable prediction file and an immutable run sidecar for every
condition. The run record carries a run identifier, split, seed, model identifier and revision, prompt-bundle
hash, corpus hash, configuration hash, timestamps, token totals, and an execution status; the sidecar adds
the prompt files and their individual hashes. Predictions carry the claim identifier, the predicted label,
the four-way label where applicable, the evidence scope, the rationale codes, and the execution status.
Schema validation is enforced before a file is accepted.
     The corpus is frozen by a SHA-256 hash over the canonicalised abstract corpus, and the split manifests
are locked separately. The held-out split is protected by a freeze record containing the model identifier, the
corpus hash, and per-method configuration and prompt hashes; a held-out run refuses to start unless the
observed hashes match the frozen ones. This is what makes “we ran the held-out split once” a checkable
statement rather than an assurance.

5.2   Retrieval
Retrieval is BM25 (Okapi, k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75, epsilon idf floor) implemented in pure Python over
the frozen abstract corpus, with a fixed alphanumeric lower-case tokenizer and no stemming. For each
neutral question three query templates are used; the top 20 candidates per query are merged, deduplicated,
and reranked by maximum BM25 score, and at most eight passages are passed to the judge. Adjacent
sentences of one document are kept together where needed to resolve negation or units. Dense retrieval and
reciprocal-rank fusion were prepared but are not enabled in the product path; they are development-time
comparisons, and we do not report a dense retrieval result we did not measure.

5.3   Models and harness
We evaluate two model families on the full samples to satisfy RQ4 and to protect the negative result from
being an artefact of one model’s behaviour, and we repeat the pairing on a wider set of models over the
fixed subset of Section 7.4.

5.4   Failure handling and the retry policy
Three different things can go wrong, and the paper keeps them apart.
1. Transport failure. The endpoint refuses the connection or returns a retryable status. The client retries
   with exponential backoff.
2. Protocol failure. The model returns text that is not the requested JSON, cites a passage it was not
   given, or returns a label outside the allowed set. The parser rejects it. This is a parse failure, not a
   scientific disagreement.
3. Scope conflict. Both stages completed and disagree. This is a scientific outcome: the reconciled
   verdict is underdetermined, which maps to NEI.
     The first two produce an execution status of failed and are excluded from the scored metrics; the
third is scored normally. Because a failed request is not evidence about the claim, the runner retries it,
up to four attempts per claim, and drops the failed row from the prediction file between attempts so that
the resume re-claims exactly those claims. The policy is applied identically to every condition and every
model family on the development sample.
     Because dropping failed rows could flatter a method that fails on the hard claims, we report a second,
stricter accounting in which every submitted row is scored with the label the method recorded for it. On
the fail-closed paths that label is the conservative NEI, so the strict accounting can only hurt a method.
Table 5 shows that the primary comparison does not depend on the choice.
     One asymmetry remains: the held-out run was always planned as a single execution and does not use
the retry policy, so its fail-closed count includes unrecovered execution failures.

5.5   Prompt contracts
Each role has a fixed output contract, expressed in the prompt and enforced by the parser. The reader
must return an answer and the identifiers of the passages it used; the judge must return one of four labels,
rationale codes, cited span identifiers, and uncertainty reasons; the citation auditor must return a label
plus a per-citation adequacy rating from {adequate, inadequate, unverifiable} and a defect list. The
parsers reject a label outside the allowed set, a cited identifier that was not supplied, and any missing
field. Appendix A states each contract; the prompt text itself is in the repository, versioned and hashed.


6     Experimental Setup
6.1   Data and splits
We use SciFact [Wadden et al., 2020]. The corpus is the released set of biomedical abstracts, frozen by
hash. The released training claims are split into a calibration subset of 100 claims, reserved before any
prompt work, and a development pool of 709 claims. The public development split (300 claims) is used
as a local held-out evaluation set; it is not an untouched community test set, and we name it accordingly
throughout. Official test labels are not used.
    Two samples are frozen by manifest: a 300-claim development sample drawn with a fixed seed from
the training pool (dev300_seed42.json under data/manifests/), used for all development
and prompt decisions, and a 300-claim evaluation sample consisting of SciFact’s public development split,
held out locally and used once under the freeze record. Both samples group claims by cited-document
family so that paraphrases and derivative claims do not straddle the comparison.

6.2   Conditions
All conditions share the frozen reports, the corpus snapshot, the claim sample, the scoring code, and the
prompt files. They differ only in the intervention named in Table 2.
    B0 measures how much the model answers from parametric knowledge alone and acts as a floor. O
supplies the gold rationale sentences to the same claim-visible judge and is a diagnostic ceiling, not a
deployable competitor. V-D1 is the D1 arm of V, recovered from the V predictions by a mapping step
rather than a second model run, so it inherits V’s failure modes by construction.

6.3   Metrics
Verification quality is measured by per-class precision, recall and F1, plus micro and macro F1 on the
native three-way label space. The primary metric is false endorsement: the number of claims predicted
SUPPORT whose gold label is not support, divided by the number of gold non-support claims. We report
the second denominator (false endorsements over all predicted SUPPORT claims) as well, because the
two answer different questions. Coverage is the fraction of submitted claims that produced a scored
prediction; fail-closed rows are counted and reported, never dropped silently. Calibration is measured
with multiclass Brier score, negative log-likelihood, and expected calibration error, fitted with a single
temperature on the calibration split only. Cost is reported as calls per claim, measured tokens per call,
and per-call latency, because a resumed run keeps only its last invocation’s usage record and per-claim
token totals are therefore not recoverable.

6.4   Statistics and preregistration
One primary comparison was preregistered before the held-out run: V versus B2 on false endorsement with
the gold non-support denominator. The interval is a paired cluster bootstrap over cited-document families,
2000 resamples, 95% percentile interval; a claim enters the comparison only when every compared
method produced an execution-ok, gold-labelled row, and dropped claims are counted. Secondary
comparisons within a declared family are Holm-adjusted; everything else is labelled exploratory. The
factorial comparison is used to interpret the mechanism, not to claim additional significance.

7.2   The 2×2 factorial
Table 6 separates the two interventions. Evidence access is the row difference (B1 versus B3, and B2
versus V-D1); reading policy is the column difference (B1 versus B2, and B3 versus V-D1). The table
shows three effects.
    First, claim-visible judging beats evidence-first judging on the scored label space: B1 (D0, claim-
visible) beats B2 (D0, evidence-first) on micro-F1 (0.843 versus 0.747), and B3 (D1, claim-visible) beats
V-D1 (D1, evidence-first) (0.800 versus 0.757). This is the opposite of the intuition behind evidence-first
staging. Reading the evidence without the claim in view loses information that the judge needs; the
sealing buys isolation, not accuracy.
    Second, evidence access barely moves the evidence-first cell: V-D1’s false endorsement (0.016) is
close to B2’s (0.011) even though V-D1 has access to a larger evidence pool, and in the MiniMax family
the two are equal (0.033 against 0.033). Independent retrieval introduces documents that the original
claim was never calibrated against, and it does not buy accuracy.
    Third, the full V is more conservative than either arm, because the reconciler resolves scope disagree-
ment into abstention rather than choosing a side. V’s zero false endorsement is thus a property of the
reconciliation policy, not of the retrieval.

7.4   The shift across four models
Two families answer the preregistered question, but not whether the conservative shift belongs to the
design or to the two models we happened to run. To check the direction more widely we repeated the
same B2-versus-V pairing on a frozen subset of the development sample: ten SUPPORT, ten REFUTE,
and ten NEI claims, selected with seed 42 before any model was run. In total four instruction-tuned
models took part, all closed-weight. All used the same prompts, corpus, retrieval budget, and scoring
code. Table 8 lists them.
    The subset is small and we read it that way. Thirty claims can resolve a difference of roughly ten
percentage points, not the one-point false-endorsement rate the full sample was sized for. Most of
the paired intervals in Table 8 therefore collapse to a point at zero, and we do not test these rows for
significance. What the subset can show is where on the label set the conservative shift lands.
    Two regularities hold across the rows. False endorsement never rises: ∆FE is zero in four of the
four models, negative in none, and positive in none; none of the four intervals exclude zero. And the
abstention lands on contradiction rather than on support: REFUTE recall is lower under V in four of the
four models and unchanged in none, while SUPPORT recall is unchanged in three models and lower in
one. Micro-F1 falls in four of the four.
    The closed-weight rows make the pattern visible. Under B2 each of them identifies most refuted
claims; under V each of them identifies half. Support recall moves by at most one claim in the same rows.
On the full sample the loss is not exclusive to REFUTE (Table 7 shows both classes falling), so the subset
is better read as showing where the shift is concentrated than as a separate finding. The mechanism is the
same one Section 7.9 describes: when the two scopes disagree, the reconciler prefers underdetermined,
and disagreement is more common exactly where a citation states the opposite of the claim.

diffuse erosion. Support loss is concentrated the same way: of the seven supported claims the baseline
finds, six are kept by every model and one is lost by some but not all. No model gains a refutation the
baseline missed, and the reverse gain for support occurs in one model–claim pair. The models also agree

7.6   Evidence acquisition
Table 10 reports what independent retrieval actually bought. Recall@k measures how often the gold
rationale documents appear in the retrieved set; overlap measures how much of D1 was already in D0 ;
unique yield measures how often retrieval found a relevant document the original citations missed.
    These numbers are unflattering to the hypothesis that independent retrieval should help. Retrieval
finds the annotated documents most of the time (recall@k = 0.85 over the 179 claims that have annotated
evidence), but every annotated document it finds was also cited by the report: the unique yield is 0.00.
The limit is in the benchmark, not in the retriever. In SciFact the cited document set contains the annotated
document set, so a retrieval system that searches the same corpus cannot find new gold evidence; it can
only add unannotated context, and 93% of what it returns is unjudged. The independent arm therefore
cannot repair a wrong citation on this corpus, because a wrong citation is, by construction, a document
without a rationale. More retrieval cannot resolve the D0/D1 conflict, and this property bounds every
claim in this paper.

8    Discussion
Independent retrieval is not sufficient. In both model families on the development sample, and again for
the Luna family under the freeze, the staged validator did not significantly reduce false endorsement
relative to a baseline that already read the original citations. The 95% interval on the primary comparison
includes zero in all three comparisons we ran.
     Staging trades recall for precision. The sealed reader makes the system conservative: it endorses
less, and what it endorses is right more often. Reported as F1 this is a regression; reported as a precision–
coverage curve it is a usable operating point. Both reports are needed, and readers of verification papers
should demand both.
     The main obstacle is disagreement between scopes, not missing evidence. Most underdetermined
verdicts come from two readers of the same corpus reaching different conclusions, not from an empty
retrieval pool. Improving retrieval alone will not fix this. A better decision procedure for the conflict case
would help, for example evidence chain evaluation [Yang, 2026], scope-matched adjudication, or explicit
conflict reporting to the reader.
     We can say nothing about human benefit. Explanations exist in the design, and prior work shows
that explanations and confidence interact with human calibration [Zhang et al., 2020], but we ran no user
study. Appendix D gives the protocol so that the study can be run without redesigning it.

   was withdrawn and the remaining requests return a model-unavailable error. The MiniMax family
   therefore corroborates the preregistered V-versus-B2 contrast and the class breakdown, not the full
   factorial, and we report its missing rows as absent rather than imputing them.
 • One frozen held-out run. The held-out sample was run once under the freeze record, so its interval is
   a single-run estimate and the two families were not both run on it.
 • No matched-budget control was run. V spends several times the tokens of B2, so compute is a
   live rival explanation for part of the F1 gap. The planned control repeats B2’s comparison step at k
   samples and votes, matching V’s token budget; it is the first experiment we would run next. We report
   the gap with that caveat rather than claiming it is explained.
 • Retrieval is BM25 only. Dense retrieval and reciprocal-rank fusion were prepared but not enabled in
   the product path. A stronger retriever could change the D0/D1 overlap numbers.
 • No user study. See RQ3 and Appendix D.

10     Ethics and Broader Impact
The task is interpreting published abstracts, not giving medical advice, and the system is evaluated only
on a frozen research corpus. The rendered output is a label, a rationale, and a search scope; it is not a
clinical recommendation and the interface says so.
    The failure mode we measured is endorsement without evidence. Our system reduces that risk by
abstaining, but abstention has its own risk: a reader who sees an abstention may infer that the claim is
false, which would be a new error. We therefore keep the scientific verdict (“underdetermined”) separate
from the system action (“defer to a reviewer”), and we recommend that any deployment display them
separately.
    The agent harness used for the MiniMax family and for the open-weight rows of the sweep is an
interactive coding agent runtime; a research run inside it inherits that runtime’s permissions and preamble.
We ran it with a fixed preamble, a fixed corpus, and no tool-enabled tasks. The model itself was withdrawn
from its free endpoint during the study, which is why the MiniMax family’s matrix is partial; we report
the conditions that completed and do not fill the rest by substitution.


11     Conclusion
We built a staged, evidence-first validator for scientific claims: a sealed reader, a claim-visible judge
over four labels, a citation auditor over the original references, and a reconciler that reports two evidence
scopes separately, with fail-closed execution semantics throughout. Against a strong same-evidence
baseline, on a frozen corpus, with locked splits, a preregistered primary comparison, and two model
families, the validator did not significantly reduce false endorsement. It became a conservative checker
whose errors come mostly from disagreement between two readers of the same literature, and the same
direction appears when the pairing is repeated across the wider set of models we could run: contradictions
are withheld, endorsements are not gained. We report that as a negative result, with the mechanism,
the cost, and the artifact needed to re-run it. The next step is a better decision procedure for scope
disagreement.

A    Prompts and output contracts
The prompts are versioned files under prompts/; the run sidecar records each file’s SHA-256 and the
bundle hash. The contracts are:

     Reader (claim-blind). Input: a neutral question and a list of passages with identifiers. Output:
     {"answer": str, "cited_doc_ids": [int]}. The reader is not shown the claim, the
     asserted answer, the report, or any gold rationale.
      Judge (claim-visible). Input: the atomic claim, the sealed reader record, and the passages.
      Output:         {"label": one of supported, contradicted, unaddressed,
      underdetermined; "rationale_codes": [str]; "cited_span_ids":
      [str]; "uncertainty_reasons": [str]}.
      Citation auditor (D0 only). Input: the atomic claim and the original cited passages. Output: the judge
      contract plus {"citations": [{"span_id": str, "adequacy": adequate |
      inadequate | unverifiable, "defects": [str]}]}.
     Compare (B2). Input: the claim, the neutral question, and the sealed reader record. Output:
     {"label": SUPPORT | REFUTE | NEI, "rationale": str}.

    The exact text of every prompt, and the parser that enforces each contract, are in the repository. The
parsers reject an out-of-set label, a cited identifier that was not supplied, and a missing mandatory field;
each rejection produces a failed execution status rather than a repaired label.

C    Calibration details
One temperature is fitted on the calibration split by minimising multiclass negative log-likelihood over
the class scores of the one condition that verbalises confidence. NoP parameter is fitted on development
or held-out data. Multiclass Brier score is the mean over claims of k (pk − ⊮[k = y])2 ; ECE uses ten
equal-width bins on the predicted probability of the argmax class. The fitted parameters, the class counts,
and the raw-versus-calibrated comparison are in the repository. We report that numerical calibration is
validated only for the condition that emits scores; for the staged validator, which emits labels only, we
report the disagreement-based selective-use analysis instead and state that its numerical calibration is
unvalidated.

D     Protocol for the deferred human study
No participant was recruited and no ethics review was obtained, so this paper makes no claim about
human benefit. The protocol is recorded here so it can be executed without redesign and so that the
absence of results is explicit.

Design. Thirty non-expert adults (a feasible range of 24–36), plus four to six pilot participants, would
each see twelve short, expert-reviewed items that retain the scientific qualifiers and span supported,
contradicted, and unresolved evidence. Each participant sees each item once. Condition assignment is
counterbalanced across participants so that item difficulty is not confounded with interface condition:
(A) conclusion plus original citations; (B) A plus the validator verdict and its uncertainty; (C) B plus the
evidence-linked explanation. Conditions B and C must use the same system verdict and confidence. Four
items per condition per participant, with randomised order and recorded assignment.

Outcomes. An initial 0–100 judgement that the conclusion is adequately supported, the same judgement
after the assigned interface, a four-way evidence-status choice, one comprehension question, a use-or-
defer decision, and response time. Expert gold support is defined as supported versus not established;
“not established” is not described to participants as factual falsity. The primary exploratory outcome is
the post-interface Brier score for evidence support comparing C with B while adjusting for pre-interface
belief and item effects; secondary outcomes are appropriate acceptance of supported claims, rejection
or deferral of unsupported claims, four-way accuracy, and overreliance when the validator is wrong.
Naturally incorrect validator outputs are included in a prespecified error stratum, reported separately from
natural error prevalence; any intentionally manipulated advice would require ethics review and debriefing.

Analysis. A mixed-effects model with participant and item random effects if the sample supports it,
otherwise participant-level paired summaries with explicit limits on item generalisation. Responses are
not treated as independent participants. The study is exploratory unless a prospective power analysis
supports stronger claims.

F    Reproduction
1. Install the package and its development extras, and verify the pinned corpus hash (data/pins/
   scifact/).
2. Start the model endpoint. For the Luna family, set OPENAI_API_KEY and OPENAI_MODEL.
   For the second family, start a local agent server and the loopback bridge in tools/opencode_
   bridge.py, then pass --base-url and --model-id to the runners.
3. Run the conditions on the development sample (scripts/run_api_dev300.sh for the Luna
   family, scripts/run_minimax_dev300.sh for the MiniMax family, which also shows the
   bridge setup). The MiniMax family’s registered conditions are the three that completed; its remaining
   conditions returned a model-unavailable error, as noted in Section 9.
4. Run the held-out sample once with --frozen-final; the runner refuses to start unless the model,
   prompt, configuration, and corpus hashes match docs/freeze.json.
5. Reproduce the cross-model sweep with scripts/make_sweep_sample.py (builds the frozen
   thirty-claim subset from the development manifest and the pinned gold release) and scripts/
   run_model_sweep.sh (runs B2 and V for each registered model, resumably, and stops the
   closed-weight models at a spend cap).
6. Regenerate every table and figure (scripts/paper/make_tables.py, scripts/paper/
   make_figures.py) and rebuild the PDF (scripts/paper/build_pdf.sh).
    Every number in this paper is read from the generated LaTeX fragments under paper/tables/,
which are produced from the scored metric JSON files under docs/paper-assets/tables/. The
prose macros in paper/tables/macros.tex are generated by the same script, so a number in the
text cannot drift from the artifact.
